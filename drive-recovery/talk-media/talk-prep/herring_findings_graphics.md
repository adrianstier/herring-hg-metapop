# Coupled Tipping Points in Pacific Herring & Haida Gwaii
### Findings → Suggested Graphics · Royal Society Session 5 · 20 May 2026

This maps each load-bearing claim in the talk to a graphic that could carry it. The organizing logic is the three-layer framework: every empirical result is positioned in the layer it speaks to, so the slide sequence *is* the argument.

---

## The argument in one frame

**Thesis.** Ecological tipping points and ecosystem-service tipping points are empirically separable. Services have their own breakpoints that lead, lag, or decouple from ecological state change. The gap between them is the interval in which management still has leverage.

**Three layers.** Ecological state → biophysical service delivery → economic and social value. Each layer can tip on its own schedule. Hysteresis is the mechanism that makes the gaps consequential: a recovered ecological state does not retrace its path back through the service and value layers.

**Three structural predictions (the spine of the abstract):**

1. **Separability.** Service-delivery breakpoints are not coincident with ecological-state breakpoints — they lead, lag, or decouple.
2. **Hysteresis.** Recovery of ecological state does not restore service delivery along the collapse path; service recovery lags ecological recovery and may not occur on management-relevant timescales.
3. **Management window.** The temporal offset between layer breakpoints is a measurable interval; the herring case shows what the system looks like after that window has closed.

> **Suggested graphic — the master schematic.** Three stacked trajectories sharing one time axis (1950→present): ecological state, service delivery, economic value. Each breaks at a different x-position. Shade the horizontal interval between the ecological breakpoint and the service breakpoint and label it *management window*. Add a faint dashed "recovery path" under the ecological curve that does **not** climb back through the service/value curves — that single dashed line is hysteresis. This is the figure every other slide hangs off; build it first and return to it as a recurring spine.

## Layer 0 — The reference state

### 1. The deepest documented baseline for any forage fish in the Northeast Pacific

McKechnie et al. (2014, *PNAS*): 435,777 fish bones from 171 Northwest Coast sites, oldest assemblage 10,700 cal BP. Herring is 49% of all identified bones, present at 169 of 171 sites (99% ubiquity), top-ranked taxon in 56% of assemblages. Within 50 well-sampled stratified sites, temporal variance in herring relative abundance is under ±10% in 96% of cases. Ten millennia of consistent superabundance — and a place-based Haida stewardship system (kin-owned harvest sites, unharvested "kelp gardens," the teaching never to take a whole school) that operated across that span.

> **Suggested graphic.** A horizontal deep-time band, 10,700 BP → present, with herring relative abundance as a near-flat ribbon at ~50% holding steady for the full pre-industrial span, then collapsing hard at the right edge. The visual point is the *flatness* — set against it, the industrial era reads as a single anomalous event, not one fluctuation among many. Optional inset: stacked-bar taxon composition at a representative site (Nulu 85% herring, Teeshoshum 93%) to make "superabundance" concrete.

## Layer 1 — Ecological state

### 2. The 1967 collapse: the clean aggregate-biomass tipping point

The BC reduction fishery scaled from ~25,000 tons (1935) to ~240,000 tons (1963). Coastwide catches then fell three seasons running — 241k → 181k → 135k tons (1964–66) — with every textbook leading indicator present: spatial contraction, rising search effort, reduced spawn deposition, truncated age structure. The fishery collapsed and was closed in 1967, shut 1968–71. DFO scientists had warned for years before political will arrived.

> **Suggested graphic.** Coastwide catch time series 1935–1972. Annotate the three-season decline, shade the 1968–71 closure, and flag the four leading indicators as a callout stack on the descending limb. This is your "tipping points have early-warning signals, and we ignored them" slide — it earns the precaution argument before you complicate it.

### 3. Portfolio erosion: the ecological tipping point that has no single date

Stier et al. (2020, *Ecosphere*), 65-year time series: synchrony among nine focal subpopulations has risen more than 60% since 1994, dismantling the asynchrony that historically kept the metapopulation 2.1× more stable than a homogeneous one. Seven of nine subpopulations show major declines in realized growth rate. Local harvest rates reached 65% even when the archipelago-wide rate was 4% — aggregate management masked local extraction.

> **Suggested graphic.** Two panels. Left: the asynchrony index, 1960s→2020, declining toward zero and crossing a marked threshold band around 1994 — a *gradual* tipping point, deliberately not a cliff. Right: nine subpopulation biomass trajectories shown as small multiples or a spaghetti plot, visibly fanning out in the early decades and converging into a single braided line after 1994. The convergence is the tipping point; the structure was the service.

### 4. The ecological tipping point is itself driven by a food-web tipping point

Stier et al. (2020) explicitly reject climate forcing and larval dispersal as drivers of the synchrony shift. The mechanism is mortality from highly mobile recovering predators — humpback whales — that move fast enough across subpopulations to homogenize their dynamics. One tipping point nested inside another.

> **Suggested graphic.** A compact causal-chain diagram: *humpback recovery → mobile, spatially coupled predation → loss of subpopulation asynchrony → portfolio collapse → metapopulation destabilization*. Show the two rejected drivers (climate, dispersal) as struck-through branches off the chain. Keep it to five nodes — this is a transition slide, not a model figure.

### 5. Recovering predators now consume more herring than the historical fishery did

North Pacific humpbacks rebounded from ~1,000 in the 1970s to 25,000–33,000 by 2021; BC abundance more than doubled, 2,145 (2006) → 4,833 (2022). Doherty (2025, *ICES JMS*) bioenergetic models: humpbacks account for 62% of all Pacific herring predation mortality and consume more herring than historical commercial fisheries combined. Natural mortality in southern Haida Gwaii subpopulations has roughly doubled since 1973.

> **Suggested graphic.** A stacked-area chart of herring mortality by source through time, with the commercial-fishery band shrinking and the humpback-predation band growing past it. The crossover point — where natural predation overtakes the fishery — is the annotation that matters. It reframes the policy problem: closing the fishery no longer controls the dominant mortality term.

## Layer 1 ↔ 2 — The management bridge

### 6. Egg and adult harvest are not the same lever — and the two "safe spaces" can overlap

Shelton, Samhouri, Stier & Levin (2014, *Sci. Reports*): herring stocks withstand substantially higher egg (spawn-on-kelp) harvest before depletion than adult harvest. Critically, ecosystem thresholds set to protect herring predators do not necessarily constrain the fishery more tightly than the fishery's own conventional targets — the safe operating space for fishers and for conservationists can be similar rather than in conflict.

> **Suggested graphic.** A two-axis operating-space diagram: egg harvest on one axis, adult harvest on the other, with the depletion-risk contour drawn across it. Overlay two regions — "fishery target space" and "predator-protective space" — and shade their intersection. The overlap is the finding: this is the slide that says management windows are not always zero-sum, which sets up Kubiszewski's ecological-economics frame.

## Layer 3 — Economic value

### 7. The economic-value tipping point lags the ecological one by roughly a decade

The coastwide roe-herring fishery hit its all-time landed-value peak (~$40M) in 1993 — in the same window the metapopulation was ecologically collapsing. Spawn-on-kelp wholesale price then fell from $40/lb (1995) to under $6/lb (2004), an 85% market collapse driven by Russian and Alaskan competition and shifting Japanese demand. By 2006, coastwide roe and SOK landed value had fallen ~93% from the 1993 peak. Aggregate biomass and aggregate value were no longer linked the way fisheries economics assumes.

> **Suggested graphic — the keystone slide.** A dual-axis time series: ecological state (synchrony / metapopulation stability) on the left axis, landed value on the right, 1985–2010. The value curve *peaks* while the ecological curve is already falling, then collapses ~10 years later. Annotate the horizontal gap between the two breakpoints as the empirical measurement of separability — Prediction 1 made visible. Tagline for the slide: *the aggregate landed value is the last thing to fall.*

## Layer 2 — Service delivery (cultural)

### 8. The cultural-service threshold has been crossed for the first time in ~10,000 years

Burnaby Narrows, Skidegate Inlet, Louscoone Inlet, Cumshewa, Selwyn, Rennell Sound, Hunter Point, Marble Island, Scudder Point, Juan Perez, Skincuttle — historically productive *k'aaw* sites now functionally closed for traditional harvest. This is not a price signal or a stock metric; it is the failure of a service that the deep baseline (Finding 1) had sustained without interruption across the entire archaeological record.

> **Suggested graphic.** A map of Haida Gwaii with the named harvest sites plotted and color-coded by status — active / diminished / functionally closed — ideally with a small-multiples version showing the same map at three time slices so the closures propagate visually. Pair the map caption with the deep-time band from Finding 1: the contrast between "10,000 years" and "now" is the emotional and scientific core of the talk.

### 9. Traditional knowledge quantifies the contraction the survey data missed

Gerrard (2014, MSc) mapped Heiltsuk Traditional Ecological Knowledge of herring spawn across ~2,500 km of the BC central coast, 1940s–2000s, documenting a 7.6% per-decade spatial contraction over 70 years — the cleanest TEK-derived quantification of the cultural-service threshold's slow approach. Place names independently preserve the lost state: *Teeshoshum*, "waters white with herring spawn," at a site with no documented spawning since 1998.

> **Suggested graphic.** A decay curve of spawn spatial extent (km of coastline) vs. decade, 1940s→2000s, fitted to the 7.6%/decade rate, with the TEK data points distinguished from survey data points. The methodological point lands visually: the contraction was gradual, monotonic, and legible in TEK *before* it was legible in stock assessment. This is also the slide that justifies treating TEK as a measurement instrument, not anecdote.

## Cross-cutting — Epistemology

### 10. The system's experts hold two incompatible mental models — and you can't predict which from who they are

Stier et al. (2016, *Conservation Letters*): fuzzy cognitive maps from 27 regional experts cluster into two structurally distinct mental models of the Haida Gwaii herring food web. The clusters are unrelated to demographic background, years of experience, or institutional affiliation. Under a simulated herring recovery, one cluster predicts 182% greater ecosystem reorganization, 78% higher average abundance change, and 89% greater whale increase than the other; the two even disagree on the *direction* of response for pink and chum salmon.

> **Suggested graphic.** Two cognitive-map networks side by side, same node set, visibly different edge structure — one sparse, one densely cross-linked. Beneath them, a small paired-bar panel of the three divergence metrics (182% / 78% / 89%). The takeaway annotation: the disagreement is not noise to be averaged away and not explained by who people are — it is a structural feature of the social layer, and a tipping point in its own right.

## Cross-cutting — Governance

### 11. Consensus co-governance as a structural intervention on the coupled system

The Archipelago Management Board, established by the 1993 Gwaii Haanas Agreement and expanded under the 2010 Marine Agreement: six seats, three federal and three Haida, decisions by consensus rather than majority, with 50% Indigenous representation. The 2024 *iináang | iinang* Pacific Herring Ecosystem-Based Rebuilding Plan — approved jointly by the Minister, the Council of the Haida Nation, and Parks Canada — is the first ecosystem-based rebuilding plan of its kind in BC. To date the majority of AMB decisions reach consensus; none has required ministerial escalation.

> **Suggested graphic.** A clean institutional schematic of the AMB: six seats, the two co-chairs, the consensus rule, and the escalation pathway (abeyance → parties → Minister) drawn as a flow. Optionally overlay it on the governance timeline so the viewer sees co-governance becoming the *default mode* across the archipelago. This is the slide that says the management window, where it still exists, is being used.

## Cross-system test

### 12. The Strait of Georgia counterfactual: the dynamics are mechanism-specific

Only one of BC's five major herring stocks remains commercially open — the Strait of Georgia, 2024 spawning biomass ~100,000+ tonnes, 2023/24 catch ~7,000 tonnes. SoG supports a resident, non-migratory population with age-0 juveniles present year-round; it acts as a metapopulation source, historically exporting ~12,000 t/yr to the other four stocks. Industrial fishing never concentrated there the same way and humpback predation pressure is structurally different. The contrast confirms that Haida Gwaii's coupled tipping-point dynamics are not a universal property of forage fish.

> **Suggested graphic.** A two-column comparison panel — Haida Gwaii vs. Strait of Georgia — with matched rows: stock status, current biomass, residency vs. migration, predation structure, fishery history. Let the rows line up so the eye reads the difference. This is the slide that immunizes the argument against "you just picked a collapsed stock": the framework predicts *where* coupling happens, and SoG is the negative control.

## Synthesis

### 13. Zero harvest, and biomass still below the limit — the window has closed

The 2025 Haida Gwaii spawning-biomass forecast was 7,556 t, below the Limit Reference Point of 6,452 t with 37.8% probability *even in the absence of fishing*. None of the proposed management procedures — including "no fishing" — keeps spawning biomass above the LRP with high probability. The harvest recommendation was zero.

> **Suggested graphic.** The 2025 forecast as a probability distribution, with the LRP drawn as a vertical line and the 37.8% probability mass below it shaded. Then a second small panel showing several management procedures (including "no fishing") as overlapping distributions — none of them clearing the LRP cleanly. The visual argument closes the talk: once a coupled system has crossed the service threshold, the fishery is no longer the lever. That is the cost of a management window left unused.

## Closing positioning note

The herring case is the empirical spine. Two comparative systems sharpen it without competing for airtime:

- **Moorea (MCR LTER) — the COVID natural experiment.** Tourism collapse drove a fishing-pressure spike while reef-fish biomass held, with real livelihood data attached. Use it, if at all, as a *fast, clean test* of the management-window concept — one slide, not a second case study.
- **Kelp forests (SBC LTER) — the fast-turnover counterfactual.** Kelp recovers on a timescale that herring and reefs do not, which is exactly what makes it useful: it demonstrates the *mechanism* of the three-layer framework rather than a competing collapse narrative. Framing it as a negative result weakens the talk; framing it as the control strengthens it.

Strategic note for Session 5: the economic-value slide (Finding 7) and the operating-space slide (Finding 6) are the bridge to Kubiszewski's ecological-economics frame. Dropping kelp as a primary system keeps clear water between this talk and White's and Smale's.
